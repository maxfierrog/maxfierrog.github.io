Abstract

In performative prediction, deploying a model changes the distribution it was trained on – predictions are self-fulfilling or self-defeating. Perdomo et al. (2020) formalize this through a distribution map \(\mathcal{D}(\theta)\) and study when repeated risk minimization converges to a stable point.1 In this post I argue that the same structure is naturally a feedback control system. I extend the distribution map with an exogenous world state \(w(t)\), decompose its time evolution via the chain rule, and cast the optimizer as a continuous-time feedback controller. Linearizing around the equilibrium yields a state-space representation \((A, E, C, F)\) whose state matrix \(A = -\alpha H\) is the product of the learning rate and the performative Hessian. The autonomous special case recovers the paper’s convergence results as Lyapunov stability; the non-autonomous case opens the door to robustness analysis (input-to-state stability, steady-state tracking error) and transient response characterization. Crucially, the Jacobian \(J_\theta = \frac{\partial \mathcal{D}}{\partial \theta} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}\) – the sensitivity of the distribution to the model – is revealed as the interface of a Stackelberg game between the learner and a strategic population, and its structure feeds into the eigenvalues of \(A\) that determine whether the retraining loop converges, oscillates, or diverges.

Background

Performative Prediction

Perdomo et al.1 consider a learner who deploys a model parameterized by \(\theta \in \Theta\). The act of deployment changes the data distribution, so the distribution is itself a function of the deployed parameters.

Definition

Distribution map. A map \(\mathcal{D}: \Theta \to \Delta(\mathcal{Z})\) that assigns to each parameter vector \(\theta\) the distribution \(\mathcal{D}(\theta)\) over the data space \(\mathcal{Z}\) that arises when \(\theta\) is deployed.

The learner’s objective is to minimize a risk functional that depends on the deployed distribution. The performative risk is

$$ \begin{equation} \text{PR}(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{Z \,\sim\, \mathcal{D}(\theta)} \!\Big[\, \ell(\theta, Z) \,\Big], \end{equation} $$

where \(\ell\) is a loss function. This creates a circular dependency: the risk depends on the distribution, which depends on the parameters, which the learner chooses by minimizing the risk.

Definition

Performative stability. A point \(\theta_{\text{PS}}\) is performatively stable if it is a fixed point of the map \(\theta \mapsto \arg\min_{\theta'} \, \mathbb{E}_{Z \sim \mathcal{D}(\theta)}[\ell(\theta', Z)]\). In other words, \(\theta_{\text{PS}}\) is optimal for the distribution it induces.

Definition

Performative optimality. A point \(\theta_{\text{PO}}\) is performatively optimal if it minimizes the performative risk directly: \(\theta_{\text{PO}} = \arg\min_{\theta} \, \text{PR}(\theta)\). Note that performative optimality implies performative stability, but not vice versa.

The paper’s central analytical tool is a Lipschitz condition on the distribution map.

Definition

\(\varepsilon\)-sensitivity. The distribution map \(\mathcal{D}\) is \(\varepsilon\)-sensitive if, for all \(\theta_1, \theta_2 \in \Theta\),

$$ d_{\mathcal{W}}\!\big(\mathcal{D}(\theta_1),\, \mathcal{D}(\theta_2)\big) \;\leq\; \varepsilon \, \| \theta_1 - \theta_2 \|, $$

where \(d_{\mathcal{W}}\) is a suitable metric on distributions.2

With \(\varepsilon\)-sensitivity and strong convexity of the loss, the paper shows that repeated risk minimization (RRM) – the procedure of alternately deploying \(\theta_t\) and retraining on \(\mathcal{D}(\theta_t)\) – converges geometrically to the performatively stable point whenever \(\varepsilon\) is small enough relative to the strong convexity constant. This is the result I want to recover from a control perspective.

Control Theory Preliminaries

I only need a small vocabulary from control theory. A continuous-time dynamical system has the form

$$ \dot{x} = f(x, u, w), $$

where \(x\) is the state, \(u\) is a control input, and \(w\) is an exogenous disturbance. A feedback controller is a policy \(u = \kappa(x)\) that closes the loop, yielding an autonomous system \(\dot{x} = f(x, \kappa(x), w)\).

An equilibrium \(x^*\) of the autonomous system (with \(w = 0\)) is Lyapunov stable if trajectories starting near \(x^*\) stay near it, and asymptotically stable if they converge to it. The standard tool for proving stability is a Lyapunov function \(V(x)\) – a positive definite, continuously differentiable function whose time derivative \(\dot{V} = \nabla V \cdot f\) is negative definite along trajectories.3

When the disturbance is nonzero (\(w \neq 0\)), one asks for input-to-state stability (ISS): the state remains bounded by a function of the disturbance magnitude. ISS gives a clean way to talk about robustness – how much the system’s behavior degrades as a function of the perturbation.

For analysis near an equilibrium, one typically linearizes and works in state-space form. If \(x \in \mathbb{R}^n\) is the state centered at the equilibrium, the linearized dynamics are

$$ \dot{x} = Ax + Bu, $$

where \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) is the state matrix and \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}\) maps inputs to state changes. The equilibrium is asymptotically stable if and only if every eigenvalue of \(A\) has strictly negative real part. The eigenvalues – the system’s poles – encode everything about the local behavior: real parts set convergence rates, imaginary parts set oscillation frequencies.

Observation

There is already a suggestive correspondence:

Control theory Performative prediction
State vector \(x \in \mathbb{R}^n\) Model parameters \(\theta \in \mathbb{R}^n\)
Control input \(u\) Parameter update \(\dot{\theta}\)
Feedback law \(u = \kappa(x)\) Optimizer / learning rule
State matrix \(A\) (to be constructed)
Equilibrium \(x^* = 0\) Performatively stable point \(\theta_{\text{PS}}\)
Eigenvalues of \(A\) Convergence rates of RRM
Disturbance \(w\) (no analogue in the paper)

The goal of the next section is to make this precise.

Formulation

The State Space

I take the model parameters \(\theta(t) \in \mathbb{R}^n\) as the state vector and the world state \(w(t) \in \mathbb{R}^m\) as an exogenous input. The distribution \(\mathcal{D}\) lives in some infinite-dimensional space, but I represent it through a finite-dimensional parameterization so that the distribution map takes the form4

$$ \begin{equation} \mathcal{D}: \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^d, \quad (\theta, w) \mapsto \mathcal{D}(\theta, w). \end{equation} $$

This is the first departure from the paper: the distribution depends on both the learner’s parameters and an exogenous world state. The sensitivity of the distribution to each input is a linear map. I define the Jacobian matrices

$$ J_\theta(\theta, w) \;=\; \frac{\partial \mathcal{D}}{\partial \theta} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}, \qquad J_w(\theta, w) \;=\; \frac{\partial \mathcal{D}}{\partial w} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times m}, $$

so that the chain rule gives the distribution dynamics in vector form:

$$ \begin{equation} \dot{\mathcal{D}} \;=\; J_\theta \, \dot{\theta} \;+\; J_w \, \dot{w} \;+\; \eta(t), \end{equation} $$

where \(\eta(t) \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is unmodeled distributional drift.5 Read this as: the distribution’s velocity is the sum of three vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) – the learner’s influence \(J_\theta \dot{\theta}\) (the control input mapped through the plant), the world’s influence \(J_w \dot{w}\), and noise.

The Strategic Jacobian

The matrix \(J_\theta \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}\) in $(3)$ deserves scrutiny. So far I have written it as a given property of the world – like the transfer function of a motor or a heating element. But in most applications of performative prediction, this Jacobian is not a passive physical quantity. It is the aggregate result of strategic agents reacting to the deployed model.

Consider strategic classification. A lender deploys a credit scoring model \(\theta\). Applicants observe (or infer) the model and adjust their features – some genuinely improve (taking a financial literacy course), others game (shifting debt between accounts). The post-deployment distribution \(\mathcal{D}(\theta)\) is the pushforward of the underlying population through a strategic response:

$$ \begin{equation} \mathcal{D}(\theta) \;=\; \text{BR}(\,\cdot\,,\, \theta)_{\#}\, \mathcal{D}_0, \end{equation} $$

where \(\mathcal{D}_0\) is the pre-deployment distribution and \(\text{BR}(z, \theta)\) is the agent’s best response – the features they choose to present given their true features \(z\) and the deployed model \(\theta\). The Jacobian then factors as

$$ J_\theta \;\sim\; \frac{\partial \, \text{BR}}{\partial \theta} \cdot \mathcal{D}_0, $$

which depends on the agents’ cost of manipulation, their rationality, their knowledge of \(\theta\), and their objectives. The learner is a Stackelberg leader committing to \(\theta\); the population is the follower best-responding to it.

This means the “plant” in our control system is not a fixed piece of physics. It is an adaptive entity whose transfer matrix depends on who is on the other side and what they want. Three qualitative regimes are worth distinguishing:

  • Cooperative. Agents’ responses genuinely reduce risk (e.g., applicants actually becoming more creditworthy). Here \(J_\theta\) reinforces the controller – the distribution shifts in a direction that helps the learner.
  • Adversarial. Agents game the system (e.g., applicants masking risk without reducing it). Here \(J_\theta\) opposes the controller – the distribution shifts to undermine the model.
  • Mixed. Some agents cooperate, some game. The columns of \(J_\theta\) have components of both types.
Observation

The paper’s \(\varepsilon\)-sensitivity condition bounds the operator norm \(\|J_\theta\|_{\text{op}} \leq \varepsilon\): it says the plant gain is at most \(\varepsilon\). The convergence condition (small \(\varepsilon\) relative to strong convexity) is a gain margin condition – the controller can stabilize the loop only if the plant gain is not too large. This is a conservative bound because it does not distinguish cooperative from adversarial components of \(J_\theta\).

Closing the Loop

The learner controls \(\dot{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^n\). Define the performative gradient

$$ g(\theta, w) \;=\; \nabla_\theta \, \text{PR}\!\big(\theta;\, \mathcal{D}(\theta, w)\big) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^n, $$

the vector pointing in the direction of steepest increase of the performative risk. The feedback law is gradient descent on PR:

$$ \begin{equation} \dot{\theta} \;=\; -\alpha \, g(\theta, w), \end{equation} $$

where \(\alpha > 0\) is the learning rate (or gain). This is a feedback law: the control input \(\dot{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) is a function of the current state through \(\mathcal{D}\) and PR.

The Closed-Loop System

Substituting the feedback law $(5)$ into the chain rule $(3)$ gives the closed-loop distribution dynamics:

$$ \begin{equation} \dot{\mathcal{D}} \;=\; -\alpha \, J_\theta \, g(\theta, w) \;+\; J_w \, \dot{w} \;+\; \eta(t). \end{equation} $$

Read this as: the distribution’s velocity is the stabilizing action of the controller (the matrix \(J_\theta\) mapping the negative gradient \(-\alpha g\) into \(\mathbb{R}^d\)), plus the world’s disturbance, plus noise.

Linearization

The nonlinear system $(5)$–$(6)$ is the full model. To apply the eigenvalue machinery of state-space analysis, I linearize around a performatively stable point \(\theta_{\text{PS}}\) with \(g(\theta_{\text{PS}}, w_0) = 0\). Define the deviation \(x = \theta - \theta_{\text{PS}} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) and the matrices

$$ H \;=\; \frac{\partial g}{\partial \theta}\bigg|_{\theta_{\text{PS}}} \!\in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}, \qquad G_w \;=\; \frac{\partial g}{\partial w}\bigg|_{\theta_{\text{PS}}} \!\in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}. $$

The matrix \(H\) is the performative Hessian – it captures how the gradient vector changes as the parameters move, including the indirect effect through the distribution. \(G_w\) is the sensitivity of the gradient to the world state. Taylor-expanding the gradient and substituting into $(5)$:

$$ \begin{equation} \dot{x} \;=\; \underbrace{-\alpha H}_{A} \, x \;+\; \underbrace{(-\alpha G_w)}_{E} \, \delta w. \end{equation} $$

This is the state equation. The distribution deviation is the output:

$$ \begin{equation} \delta\mathcal{D} \;=\; \underbrace{J_\theta\vphantom{G}}_{C} \, x \;+\; \underbrace{J_w\vphantom{G}}_{F} \, \delta w. \end{equation} $$

Equations $(7)$ and $(8)$ are the state-space representation \((A, E, C, F)\) of the performative prediction loop. Every question about stability, robustness, and transient behavior reduces to a question about these four matrices.

Note

The state matrix \(A = -\alpha H\) is the product of the scalar gain \(\alpha\) and the performative Hessian \(H\). The Hessian itself depends on the distribution coupling through \(J_\theta\) – so the strategic structure of the Jacobian is baked into the eigenvalues of \(A\). I will unpack this in the Analysis.

The Autonomous Special Case

Setting \(\delta w = 0\) (no world drift) recovers the paper’s setting:

$$ \begin{equation} \dot{x} \;=\; Ax \;=\; -\alpha H x. \end{equation} $$

Asymptotic stability requires every eigenvalue of \(A\) to have strictly negative real part – equivalently, every eigenvalue of \(H\) must have strictly positive real part. Under the paper’s strong convexity assumptions, \(H\) is positive definite (\(H \succ 0\)), so all its eigenvalues are real and positive, all eigenvalues of \(A\) are real and negative, and the system is exponentially stable.6 This recovers the geometric convergence of RRM.

Analysis

Stability

In the autonomous case $(9)$, the quadratic form

$$ V(x) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2} \, x^\top H \, x $$

is a natural Lyapunov function – it is the second-order approximation of \(\text{PR}(\theta) - \text{PR}(\theta_{\text{PS}})\).3 Its time derivative under the dynamics is

$$ \begin{equation} \dot{V} \;=\; x^\top H \dot{x} \;=\; -\alpha \, x^\top H^2 x \;\leq\; 0. \end{equation} $$

Since \(H \succ 0\), we have \(H^2 \succ 0\), so \(\dot{V} < 0\) for all \(x \neq 0\). The convergence rate is set by the smallest eigenvalue: if \(\lambda_1 \leq \cdots \leq \lambda_n\) are the eigenvalues of \(H\), the slowest decaying mode has rate \(\alpha \lambda_1\), and

$$ \|x(t)\| \;\leq\; \|x(0)\| \, e^{-\alpha \lambda_1 t}. $$

This recovers the paper’s geometric convergence. Under the \(\varepsilon\)-sensitivity and strong convexity assumptions, \(\lambda_1 > 0\) is guaranteed, and \(\alpha \lambda_1\) is the continuous-time analogue of the paper’s contraction factor.

The takeaway: performative stability is Lyapunov stability of the closed-loop system, and the convergence rate is determined by the smallest eigenvalue of the performative Hessian \(H\).

Robustness

When \(\delta w \neq 0\), the full state equation $(7)$ applies:

$$ \dot{x} \;=\; Ax + E\,\delta w \;=\; -\alpha H x - \alpha G_w \,\delta w. $$

The performatively stable point drifts: \(\theta_{\text{PS}}(w(t))\) becomes a moving target. ISS analysis of the \((A, E)\) pair gives:

  • Bounded tracking error. If \(\delta w\) is bounded, the tracking error \(\|x(t)\|\) is bounded by a function of \(\|\delta w\|\) and the ISS gain \(\|A^{-1}E\| = \|H^{-1}G_w\|\).
  • Steady-state error. For a constant disturbance \(\delta w\), the system settles to \(x_{\text{ss}} = -A^{-1}E\,\delta w = H^{-1}G_w\,\delta w\). The steady-state error depends on the condition number of \(H\): if \(H\) is poorly conditioned, even small world shifts cause large parameter errors along the ill-conditioned eigenvectors.
  • Recovery. If \(\delta w(t) \to 0\), the system recovers asymptotic stability and \(x(t) \to 0\).

None of this is available in the original framework, which assumes a static world. The state-space formulation gives it for free from the \((A, E)\) pair.

Transient Response

Even in the autonomous case, the path to equilibrium matters in practice. The eigenvalues of \(A = -\alpha H\) determine the transient behavior:

  • Convergence rates. The slowest mode decays at rate \(\alpha \lambda_{\min}(H)\), the fastest at \(\alpha \lambda_{\max}(H)\). A large condition number \(\kappa(H) = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\) means some directions converge much faster than others – the trajectory takes a long, narrow path to the equilibrium.
  • Overshoot. Even though the state \(x(t)\) decays monotonically in each eigendirection of \(A\) (since \(H \succ 0\) implies real eigenvalues), the distribution output \(\delta\mathcal{D}(t) = C\,x(t) = J_\theta \, x(t)\) can overshoot. The output matrix \(C = J_\theta\) mixes eigenmodes: if fast-decaying and slow-decaying components contribute with opposite signs to a particular distribution coordinate, that coordinate will overshoot before settling.
  • Oscillation. For the gradient flow controller $(5)$, the performative Hessian \(H\) is symmetric, so \(A\) has real eigenvalues and the linearized \(\theta\)-dynamics do not oscillate. But other feedback laws – repeated risk minimization, natural gradient, gradient descent with momentum – yield non-symmetric state matrices whose eigenvalues can be complex. Complex eigenvalues produce spiraling trajectories: the learner and population alternately overreacting to each other.
  • Gain tuning. The learning rate \(\alpha\) scales all eigenvalues of \(A\) uniformly: doubling \(\alpha\) doubles every convergence rate but also doubles any oscillation frequency. The condition number \(\kappa(H)\) is independent of \(\alpha\) – tuning the scalar gain cannot fix a poorly conditioned Hessian.

Strategic Coupling

The analysis above treated \(H\) as given. But \(H = \frac{\partial g}{\partial \theta}\) is the total derivative of the performative gradient, which includes the indirect effect through the distribution map. Schematically:

$$ H \;=\; H_{\text{direct}} \;+\; H_{\text{indirect}}(J_\theta), $$

where \(H_{\text{direct}} = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\nabla^2_{\theta\theta} \, \ell] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) is the curvature of the loss at fixed distribution, and \(H_{\text{indirect}}\) captures how the distribution’s response to \(\theta\) feeds back into the gradient. The strategic Jacobian \(J_\theta\) appears inside \(H_{\text{indirect}}\).

In the cooperative regime, \(H_{\text{indirect}}\) is positive semi-definite: the population’s response increases the curvature of the effective loss, and the eigenvalues of \(H\) are larger than those of \(H_{\text{direct}}\) alone. The state matrix \(A = -\alpha H\) has more negative eigenvalues, and convergence is faster.

In the adversarial regime, \(H_{\text{indirect}}\) is negative semi-definite: the population’s response flattens or inverts the effective loss landscape. If the adversarial coupling is strong enough – if \(\|H_{\text{indirect}}\|_{\text{op}} > \lambda_{\min}(H_{\text{direct}})\) – then \(H\) loses positive definiteness, \(A\) acquires a positive eigenvalue, and the system is unstable. The \(\varepsilon\)-sensitivity condition prevents this: bounding \(\|J_\theta\|_{\text{op}} \leq \varepsilon\) bounds \(\|H_{\text{indirect}}\|_{\text{op}}\), ensuring \(H \succ 0\) for small enough \(\varepsilon\).

Observation

The adversarial regime is where the state-space perspective adds the most. The paper’s framework converges or doesn’t; it has no way to describe how the population’s strategic response interacts with the optimizer along the trajectory. In the state-space formulation, this interaction is encoded in the eigenvalues of \(A = -\alpha H\): their magnitudes set convergence rates, their signs determine stability, and (for non-gradient feedback laws) their imaginary parts produce oscillation. The critical transition – where an eigenvalue of \(A\) crosses zero because the adversarial component of \(J_\theta\) overpowers the direct curvature \(H_{\text{direct}}\) – is a bifurcation in the learner-population system, invisible to the original framework.

The connection to differential games is now explicit. The learner and the population are playing a continuous-time Stackelberg game: the learner commits to a policy \(\dot{\theta} = -\alpha g(\theta, w)\), the population best-responds through \(\text{BR}(\cdot, \theta)\), and the equilibrium – if it exists – is the performatively stable point \(\theta_{\text{PS}}\). The stability analysis of the closed-loop system is the stability analysis of this game’s dynamics, and the matrices \(A\), \(C\), \(J_\theta\), and \(H\) are its structural parameters.

Discussion

The central claim of this post is a change of vocabulary. The distribution map is a plant with transfer matrix \(J_\theta\). The optimizer is a controller. Performative stability is Lyapunov stability. The convergence of RRM is asymptotic stability under feedback, governed by the eigenvalues of the state matrix \(A = -\alpha H\). When the plant is a strategic population, \(J_\theta\) is the interface of a game, and its structure is baked into \(H\). Once the vocabulary is in place, the toolbox of control theory becomes available without additional mathematical development.

What is gained:

  • Robustness. The world state \(w(t) \in \mathbb{R}^m\) and noise \(\eta(t) \in \mathbb{R}^d\) are first-class citizens. The \((A, E)\) pair answers questions like “how fast can the world change before the learner can no longer track?” – the steady-state error is \(x_{\text{ss}} = H^{-1}G_w\,\delta w\), readable directly from the matrices.
  • Transient analysis. The eigenvalues of \(A\) determine convergence rates; the condition number \(\kappa(H)\) determines the spread. The output matrix \(C = J_\theta\) determines whether the distribution overshoots even when the parameters do not.
  • Strategic structure. The decomposition \(H = H_{\text{direct}} + H_{\text{indirect}}(J_\theta)\) gives finer-grained stability conditions than \(\varepsilon\)-sensitivity alone, and connects the framework to Stackelberg games and differential games.
  • Controller design. The learning rate \(\alpha\) is a scalar gain on the state matrix. This suggests principled tuning and possibly more sophisticated controllers (adaptive gain, integral action, robust control for adversarial populations).

Limitations:

  • Continuous time is an idealization. Real retraining is discrete. The continuous-time formulation is a useful fiction5 – it simplifies analysis and builds intuition, but the discretization gap matters in practice.
  • The matrices are not observable. The Jacobians \(J_\theta\) and \(J_w\), and therefore the Hessian \(H\), are not directly measurable. In control terms, we are designing a controller without a plant model, which limits what guarantees we can provide. This is especially acute in the adversarial case, where \(J_\theta\) itself depends on the population’s strategy and may change as \(\theta\) evolves.
  • The world state is not measurable. We posited \(w(t) \in \mathbb{R}^m\) as an exogenous input, but in practice the learner may not know what \(w\) is, let alone measure it. This is an observability problem.
  • The strategic model is stylized. The best-response model $(4)$ assumes agents observe \(\theta\) and respond optimally. In practice, agents have partial information, bounded rationality, and heterogeneous objectives. The matrix \(J_\theta\) is messier than any clean decomposition suggests.

These limitations are not fatal – they are the same limitations faced by adaptive control and online learning, which have developed practical tools despite them. The connection to strategic classification is now explicit rather than suggestive: the “plant” in that setting is a population of strategic agents, \(J_\theta\) encodes their best-response behavior, and the stability of the retraining loop is the stability of a Stackelberg game. I suspect the right next step is to take a concrete instance – a linear strategic classification model with known agent cost functions – and work out the eigenvalues of \(A = -\alpha H\) explicitly, to see whether the cooperative/adversarial decomposition of \(J_\theta\) yields meaningfully tighter convergence bounds than \(\varepsilon\)-sensitivity.
